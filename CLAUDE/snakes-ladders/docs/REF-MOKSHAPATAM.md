# JOB A — Moksha Patam: the original, and how to reclaim it honestly

## A1. What the game actually was

| Name | Region / tradition | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Moksha Patam / Mokshapat** | Pan-Indian, Hindu | "liberation board" |
| **Gyan Chaupar** (gyan chaupad) | Jain, western India; later Mughal courts | "game of knowledge", played on a *chaupar* cloth |
| **Paramapada Sopanam / Sopana Patam** | Tamil Nadu (*Paramapadam*), Andhra (*Vaikuntapali*) | "steps to the highest place" |
| **Nagapasa** | Nepal | "snake-noose" |
| **Leela** | later esoteric/Tantric revival | "divine play" |

Dated to medieval India — variously attributed to the 13th-c. Marathi saint **Dnyaneshwar**, and to Jain teachers of western India around the same period; a 10th-c. reference (Dhanapala) suggests an older Jain ancestor. **Andrew Topsfield** (Keeper of Eastern Art, Ashmolean) is the definitive scholar: *"The Indian Game of Snakes and Ladders"* (Artibus Asiae, 1985) and *"Snakes and Ladders in India: Some Further Discoveries"* (2006), between them cataloguing ~44 surviving boards, the oldest late-15th century. Topsfield's summary of the crux: the spiritual quest for liberation from the vicissitudes of karma.

**Board sizes were doctrinal, not arbitrary:**

| Squares | Grid | Tradition | Why that number |
|---|---|---|---|
| **72** | 9×8 | Hindu (also Nepal) | cosmological schema |
| **84** | 9×9 + extras at 1, 46, 66 | **Jain** (the standardised form) | 84 lakh yonis — the 8.4 million births the soul cycles through |
| **100** | 10×10 | Muslim / Sufi | the 100 names of God |
| **124** | 14×10 | Maharashtra | elaborated local cosmology |
| **128** | — | (Topsfield catalogue) | — |

## A2. How the board was read

- **Boustrophedon**, bottom-left upward — the same serpentine path modern boards keep, but here it *meant* something: a single continuous ascent, not a track.
- Bottom = **Narak Dwar** (gate of hell) / lower consciousness. Middle = the human and heavenly *lokas*. Top = **Swarg**, then **Moksha**.
- The Jain reading is the most precise: the board is the **gunasthana**, the "scale of perfection" — the soul starts in *mithyatva* (total delusion) and ascends to *kevala jnana* (omniscience). The highest obstacle square on a late-18th-c. Jain board is **mohaniya karma**, deluding karma: the last thing you shake off is the desire that made you play.
- The token is the **jiva** (soul). Its movement across squares is transmigration across *lokas*. A ladder is not a shortcut — it is a **birth upward**. A snake is a **birth downward**. One game = many lifetimes.
- Randomiser: **cowrie shells or long dice**, not a cubical d6.

**Note square 69: Debt was literally a named vice square in the original.** Your game does not need to invent a financial layer — one was already there.

**The 72-square Gyan Chaupar:** bad company (24), false knowledge (44), ego (55).

**The Sanskrit sets** underlying most boards — vices: *kama* (lust), *krodha* (anger), *lobha* (greed), *moha* (delusion/attachment), *mada* (arrogance), *matsarya* (envy), *ahankara* (ego), *himsa* (violence). Virtues: *daya* (compassion), *dana* (giving), *bhakti* (devotion), *gyana* (knowledge), *tapas* (discipline), *satya* (truth), *dharma* (right action), *santosha* (contentment).

**Jain boards ran roughly 9 snakes to 5 ladders.** The asymmetry was the thesis: *a path of good is much harder to tread than a path of sins.*

## A4. The philosophy is in the mechanic, not the artwork

This is the part almost every "reclaim our heritage" retelling misses, and it is the only part that matters to you as a designer:

1. **There is no choice.** You do not *decide* to be greedy. The dice decide, and you bear it anyway. That is precisely the doctrine of karma-as-inheritance: you are living out consequence you did not consciously select this turn.
2. **Consequence is impersonal.** No judge, no scoring, no forgiveness mechanic. The square does what the square does. Karma as physics, not as punishment.
3. **Asymmetry of effort.** Long ladders are rare and high; snakes are numerous and everywhere. Falling is cheap, rising is expensive.
4. **The ending is release, not victory.** Landing exactly on the top square is *moksha* — exiting the game, not beating anyone. In the traditional festival play (Vaikuntha Ekadashi, Tamil Nadu), reaching Vaikuntha is a devotional act.
5. **The moral content is load-bearing.** The name of the vice *is* the coordinate. Remove the name and the square is arithmetic.

## A5. How the British stripped it out — in two stages, not one

**Stage 1 — Victorian moralisation (Jaques of London, c. 1892).** The structure survived; the theology was swapped for bourgeois virtue. Ladders led to **Fulfilment, Grace, Success**; snakes were **Indulgence, Disobedience, Indolence**. And critically: **the English board equalised the number of snakes and ladders.** That single change destroyed the argument. An asymmetric cosmology ("descent is easier than ascent") became a symmetric fairness machine ("the world is even-handed"). The metaphysics became a lottery.

**Stage 2 — American de-moralisation (Milton Bradley, *Chutes and Ladders*, 1943).** Snakes were removed outright — children were thought to dislike them — and replaced with **playground slides**. Vices became "a child did a naughty thing"; virtues became "a child did a nice thing", illustrated in the *margin next to* the square. Over subsequent decades even those illustrations drained to decoration.

**The precise loss, stated as a design principle:** the meaning migrated **from the mechanic to the ornament**. In Moksha Patam, "Debt" was a coordinate — the thing that moved you. In Chutes and Ladders, the reason is a picture beside a number that would move you identically if it were blank. *Any* modern reclamation that puts financial content in tooltips, popup cards, or square artwork while the numbers stay arbitrary has repeated the colonial move, just in Hindi.

**Corollary for your build: a square's financial content must determine its jump size.** If "credit card revolving at 42% p.a." doesn't drop you further than "broke your SIP for a gadget", you have made a Milton Bradley board with Indian labels.

## A6. Reclaiming it for money — without preaching

### The single structural swap that does the ethical work

**The original indicts the actor. Your version must indict the instrument.**

Moksha Patam's snakes are *character defects* — lust, rage, pride. That was coherent for a soteriology about the soul's own passions across lifetimes. Transplanted onto household finance in one lifetime, the same grammar becomes: *you are poor because you are greedy and undisciplined*. That is not a translation, it is a slander.

So: **keep the geometry, invert the subject of the sentence.**

| Don't (vice-as-character) | Do (mechanism-as-consequence) |
|---|---|
| "Greed — you chased returns" | "**F&O punt.** 9 in 10 retail traders lose. −₹40,000" |
| "Laziness — you didn't save" | "**Salary-day gap.** SIP dated the 28th; money's gone by the 20th. Move the date." |
| "Vanity — you overspent" | "**No-cost EMI.** The 'no cost' was in the sticker price. Effective 16% p.a." |
| "You fell for a scam" | "**Chit fund promised 3% a month.** It was structured to fail. SEBI-registered ≠ optional." |
| "Debt (69)" | "**Revolving credit at 42% p.a.** Minimum-due is the trap, not the balance." |

Every snake card should be readable by the person who *actually made that choice last month* without them feeling accused. Test: read it aloud to someone who did the thing. If they flinch at being described rather than informed, rewrite it.

### Rule set for the writing

1. **Second person for actions you control; third person for structures you don't.** "You paid a 2.25% trail commission" vs. "The regular plan pays the distributor 2.25% out of your returns."
2. **Rupees, never percentages of virtue.** The IFM Gen Z copy rule applies: money not %. A snake says "−₹18,400 over 10 years", not "−12% CAGR".
3. **Name the counterparty.** Every snake has someone on the other side who got paid. Insurance agent, distributor, card issuer, broker. This relocates blame from the player to the product without becoming conspiratorial — it's just accurate.
4. **No snake is a moral judgement about spending on family.** Weddings, medical care, education, parents — never a snake. Ever. This is the fastest way to lose an Indian audience and it deserves to be.
5. **The virtue squares get boring names on purpose.** "Automated the SIP", "Emergency fund at 3 months", "Switched to direct plan". Ladders in your game are *plumbing*, not righteousness.

### The ethical trap, named plainly

The critical-education literature on financial literacy is blunt: curricula **overwhelmingly adopt an individualistic paradigm of poverty**, framing financial wellbeing as "a personal doing" while neglecting the social, economic and political forces that determine who accumulates. Wrap that framing in a *karma* game — a game whose original thesis is literally that your position is deserved — and you have built the most elegant victim-blaming machine in Indian pedagogy. The aesthetic will make it feel ancient and true.

**Three concrete defences to build into the code, not the copy:**

**1. The Shock deck — consequences with no author.**
Separate from snakes, add non-blame events that hit at random: hospital admission, job loss, rent hike, crop failure, a parent's care costs. They are explicitly marked *not your fault*. They set you back. **And the Emergency Fund shield absorbs them** (see Job B, catch-up ranking — this is also the mathematically fairest catch-up mechanic on the board). The game therefore teaches *build the buffer* rather than *avoid the sin*. This is the single most important design decision in the whole project: it makes structural risk visible as a rule rather than as a disclaimer.

**2. Identical starting capital, explicitly flagged.** Every player starts square 0, same rupees. Say so on the setup screen in one line: *"Everyone starts level here. Real life doesn't — that's a different problem, and it isn't yours to fix alone."* One sentence. Then never mention it again; it does its work by being true.

**3. Never let the win condition be wealth.** The top square is *your goal funded*, not *richest player*. Honouring the original — moksha was release, not victory — and defusing comparison. A player who reaches 100 with a smaller corpus has still reached 100.

### Honest reclamation of the original coordinates

Do not invent fresh square numbers. **Reuse the traditional ones and re-tenant them** — this is the reclamation, and it is verifiable rather than decorative:

| Original square | Original meaning | Financial re-tenanting |
|---|---|---|
| **12** Faith (ladder) | trust as first step | **First SIP started.** The hardest step is the first ₹500. |
| **51** Reliability (ladder) | steadfastness | **Auto-debit on salary day.** Reliability outsourced to a machine. |
| **57** Generosity (ladder) | *dana* | **Term insurance bought.** Generosity is a payout your family gets when you can't give. |
| **76** Knowledge (ladder) | *gyana* | **Read the expense ratio.** |
| **78** Asceticism (ladder) | *tapas* | **Held through a 30% crash.** *Tapas* is now literally "did nothing for 18 months". |
| **62** Drunkenness (snake) | intoxication | **Bull-market euphoria.** Bought at the top because everyone was. |
| **69** Debt (snake) | debt | **Revolving credit at 42% p.a.** |
| **92** Greed (snake) | *lobha* | **F&O.** |
| **95** Pride (snake) | *mada* | **"I don't need advice."** — placed high on purpose, as in the original. |
| **99** Lust (snake) | *kama* | Retire this one. A square at 99 that punishes desire, one step from the goal, is the original's cruellest joke and the worst possible financial lesson. **See Job B: nothing bites above 96.** |

A short "the original board said X here" line on each square — surfaced once, not every visit — turns this from theming into an argument. And unlike the Victorian version, you are keeping the mechanic honest: *the jump sizes follow the money*.

# JOB B — Craft of a 100-square race

All figures below are computed on an absorbing Markov chain over 101 states (validated: my standard-board figure of **35.83** matches the published 35.8; my exact-finish-stay-put figure of **39.23** matches the literature's 39.2).

## B1. Baseline maths — the standard board, single d6

Standard 100-square board (9 ladders, 10 snakes: 1→38, 4→14, 9→31, 21→42, 28→84, 36→44, 51→67, 71→91, 80→100; 16→6, 47→26, 49→11, 56→53, 62→19, 64→60, 87→24, 93→73, 95→75, 98→78):

| Finish rule | E[turns] | SD | Median | Mode | p90 | p99 | Min |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Overshoot wins** (≥100 finishes) | **35.83** | 23.35 | 29 | 20 | 66 | 119 | 7 |
| Exact finish, **stay put** on overshoot | 39.23 | 25.22 | 32 | 22 | 72 | 128 | 7 |
| Exact finish, **bounce back** | 43.32 | — | 35 | 21 | 83 | **151** | 7 |
| Overshoot wins **+ roll-6-again** | 30.21 | — | 25 | 17 | 56 | 101 | **3** |
| **Empty board, no snakes/ladders** | 29.05 | **2.61** | 29 | 29 | 32 | 36 | 17 |

**The headline finding, and it is counter-intuitive:** snakes and ladders barely lengthen the game (29.1 → 35.8 turns, +23%) but they multiply the *unpredictability* by nine (SD 2.6 → 23.4). The p99 goes from 36 turns to 119. **Snakes and ladders are not a difficulty system; they are a variance system.** Every design decision below is really a decision about how much variance you want.

**Multiplayer — rounds until the first player reaches 100** (standard board, overshoot-wins):

| Players | Rounds to first finish | Total dice rolls |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 34.8 | 35 |
| 2 | 23.0 | 46 |
| 3 | 18.8 | 56 |
| **4** | **16.6** | **66** |

## B2. Why the classic board frustrates — quantified

Cost of every snake, in **turns lost** (expected bites per game × drop ÷ 3.5 average roll):

| Snake | Drop | Bites/game | **Turns lost** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **87 → 24** | 63 | 0.479 | **8.63** ← the single worst thing on the board |
| 49 → 11 | 38 | 0.563 | 6.11 |
| 47 → 26 | 21 | 0.656 | 3.93 |
| 62 → 19 | 43 | 0.252 | 3.10 |
| 93 → 73 | 20 | 0.378 | 2.16 |
| 95 → 75 | 20 | 0.372 | 2.12 |
| 16 → 6 | 10 | 0.455 | 1.30 |
| 98 → 78 | 20 | 0.223 | 1.28 |
| 64 → 60 | 4 | 0.256 | 0.29 |
| 56 → 53 | 3 | 0.313 | 0.27 |

Net board economy: **−102.2 squares** lost to snakes vs **+77.6** gained from ladders = **net −24.6 squares (≈7 turns)** — the board is rigged against you, faithfully preserving the Jain asymmetry even in the American edition.

**The heartbreak metric.** Four snake mouths sit at 87, 93, 95, 98. Expected late-game bites: **1.45 per player per game → a 77% chance that a given player gets bitten at least once while inside the last 15 squares.** That is the frustration, precisely located. It is not that the game is long; it is that **three players in four get destroyed within sight of the finish**, and 87→24 specifically sends you back two-thirds of the board after you had effectively won.

**Softening it (standard board, overshoot-wins):**

| Change | E[turns] | Median | p90 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full classic | 35.83 | 29 | 66 |
| Remove 98→78 | 32.85 | 27 | 59 |
| Cap all top-quarter snakes to 12-square drops | 29.6 | 27 | 50 |
| Cap all top-quarter snakes to 8-square drops | 28.4 | 26 | 47 |
| Remove 87→24 alone | 29.06 | 26 | 49 |
| **No snakes above square 85** | **24.62** | **22** | **41** |

How modern digital versions soften this: overshoot-wins as default, shortened end-game snakes, "shield" pickups, animation that reframes the fall as comedy rather than loss, and — most effective — ending the game at first finish rather than playing out the field.

**How many snakes and ladders?** (random boards, matched counts, 6–25 square spans)

| Pairs | E[turns] | SD of *game length* |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 29.0 | 2.6 |
| 4 | 30.3 | 8.4 |

**How long should the jumps be?** (9 ladders / 10 snakes)

| Span | E[turns] | SD |
|---|---|---|
| 3–8 | 31.3 | 5.9 |
| **6–15** | **33.7** | **11.1** |
| 10–25 | 35.7 | 17.7 |
| 20–45 | 36.2 | 25.9 |
| 30–70 | 36.1 | 29.0 |

**Span dominates count.** Going from 4 pairs to 15 pairs adds 4.5 turns; going from short spans to long spans adds 23 points of SD at essentially no change in mean. If you want drama without chaos, **add more jumps, make them shorter** — never the reverse.

**Ladder : snake ratio** (span 6–25) — this is violently non-linear:

| L : S | E[turns] | SD |
|---|---|---|
| 12 : 6 | 24.6 | 8.5 |
| 10 : 8 | 29.8 | 12.1 |
| 9 : 10 | 35.1 | 15.8 |
| 8 : 12 | 42.5 | 21.6 |
| 6 : 14 | 59.0 | 34.8 |
| 5 : 15 | **73.9** | 46.9 |

The authentic Jain 9-snakes-to-5-ladders ratio produces a ~74-turn game with SD 47. **Historically faithful, completely unplayable on a phone.** Honour the asymmetry in the *fiction* (more ways to leak money than to build it), not in the *counts*.

## B4. Recommended board — 8 ladders, 8 snakes

Spans 8–24. Nothing bites above 96, and the two late snakes drop ≤10.

**Ladders (mechanisms that compound):** 3→22, 12→30, 17→36, 25→49, 41→62, 54→71, 68→85, 78→94
**Snakes (products & situations):** 27→9, 38→20, 46→28, 57→40, 66→47, 74→55, **89→79 (drop 10)**, **96→88 (drop 8)**

| Board | Rule | E[turns] | SD | Median | p90 | p99 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | overshoot wins | 35.83 | 23.35 | 29 | 66 | 119 |
| **Recommended** | **overshoot wins** | **28.08** | **11.06** | **26** | **43** | **64** |
| Recommended | exact, stay put | 32.29 | 12.38 | 30 | 49 | 70 |
| Recommended | exact, bounce | 34.45 | 14.29 | 32 | 53 | 79 |

Net economy **+3.4 squares** (mildly generous, deliberately). Late-game heartbreak drops from **77% → 55%**, and when it happens it costs one turn, not nine. The p99 halves: 119 → 64. **You have removed the disaster tail without removing the drama** — SD is still 11, four times the empty board.

## B5. Exact finish vs. overshoot

| Rule on the recommended board | E[turns] | **Turns spent stuck in squares 95–99** |
|---|---|---|
| **Overshoot wins** | 28.08 | **1.29** |
| Exact, stay put | 32.29 | 5.44 |
| Exact, bounce back | 34.45 | 5.17 |

The exact-finish rule imposes a **~4-turn tax levied entirely in the final five squares** — at the exact moment attention and tension are highest, you make players roll and fail four times in a row. On a phone, with a 2-second dice animation each time, that is 8+ seconds of pure nothing per player.

**Recommendation: overshoot wins.** If you want the ritual of the exact landing (and there is a real case for it — *moksha* required precision), use **exact-finish with a mercy rule: after 2 failed exact attempts, any roll finishes.** Costs ~1 turn, keeps the moment, kills the tax.

## B6. House rules that speed the game up, ranked by turns saved

| Rule | Effect (standard board) | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **End at first finish; rank everyone else by square** | Removes **43.5 dead rounds** in 4p | None. Do this. |
| Overshoot wins | −3.4 turns | Loses the exact-landing ritual |
| Roll a 6 → roll again | 35.83 → **30.21** (−5.6) | Min game drops to 3 turns; raises variance |
| Cap late-snake drops at ≤10 | 35.83 → ~29.6 | Slight loss of jeopardy |
| Remove snakes above 85 entirely | 35.83 → **24.62** | End-game becomes a coin-flip-free procession |
| Everyone starts on square 10 | ~−3 turns | Free, invisible, slightly cheapening |

## B7. Keeping the behind player engaged

**First, the real problem — measured.** Rounds the *last* player keeps rolling after the *first* player has already won:

| Players | Standard board | Recommended (softened) board |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 23.8 (median 17) | 18.6 (median 14) |
| 3 | 35.8 (median 30) | 27.7 (median 24) |
| **4** | **43.5 (median 37)** | 33.8 (median 31) |

**In a 4-player standard game the loser plays for 43 more rounds after the winner is done.** This — not snake placement — is why families abandon the game mid-session. **Ending at first finish and ranking by position eliminates 100% of it for zero design cost.** Everything below is for keeping the trailing player engaged *during* the race.

**Catch-up mechanics — measured (4 players, standard board, trigger = 25+ squares behind the leader):**

| Mechanic | 1st finish | Gap 1st→last | Gap closed | **P(round-15 last place wins)** | Distortion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *None (baseline)* | 17.5 | 43.3 | — | 8.7% | — |
| **One snake immunity** (re-arms) | 14.9 | **16.0** | **−27.3** | **8.4%** | **−0.3pp** |
| Double your move | 16.2 | 26.4 | −16.9 | 10.0% | +1.3pp |
| Roll 2 dice, take best | 17.0 | 35.5 | −7.8 | 8.8% | +0.1pp |
| +1 to the die | 17.2 | 36.6 | −6.7 | 9.2% | +0.5pp |
| Reroll a 1 or 2 | 17.3 | 38.5 | −4.8 | 8.7% | 0.0pp |

(Fair share would be 25%; a trailing player *should* usually lose.)

**Ranked by fairness — gap closed per point of win-probability distortion:**

1. **🥇 Snake immunity / shield.** Closes 27 rounds of dead time while *lowering* the laggard's win rate by 0.3pp. It compresses the field without rewriting the outcome, because it prevents catastrophe rather than granting speed. Nothing else is close.
2. **🥈 Reroll a 1 or 2.** Literally zero distortion. Small effect, but it makes the trailing player's turn feel less punishing — and psychologically it's the turn *quality* that matters, not the position.
3. **🥉 Roll 2 dice, take best.** +0.1pp distortion for 7.8 rounds. Good, and it's the most legible to a 10-year-old.
4. **+1 to the die.** Fine, but strictly dominated by the 2-dice version.
5. **Double your move.** +1.3pp — the only one that meaningfully bends the result, and it feels arbitrary. Avoid.

**Why this matters beyond fairness:** the shield mechanic *is* the emergency fund. The mathematically fairest catch-up mechanic on the board is also the correct financial lesson: **the buffer doesn't make you rich, it stops you being destroyed.** Ship it as "Emergency Fund — absorbs one snake", earned by landing on the 12→30 ladder or offered when a player falls 25+ behind. Job A's ethical requirement and Job B's fairness optimum are the same mechanic. Build that one thing well and both halves of this brief are answered.

**Engagement tactics that aren't catch-up mechanics:**
- **Give the trailing player something to read.** They have the most turns and the most attention — they see more snake cards than anyone. Make the losing path the most educational path, then say so in the end screen: "You hit 6 money leaks. Ravi hit 2. Guess who can now name all six."
- **Rank by squares, always visible.** A persistent standings strip means "I'm 2nd, 4 squares off" instead of "I'm losing."
- **Nobody is eliminated, ever.** Snakes & ladders has no elimination — do not add one.
- **Personal-best framing.** "Fastest ₹ to goal" as a secondary stat gives the last-place player something to have won.

## B8. Pass-and-play on ONE phone: how it actually works

**This is the primary mode, not a fallback.** 85.5% of Indian households own at least one smartphone (Comprehensive Modular Survey: Telecom, 2025), but a majority of Indian phone users **share** their device with family and are not exclusive users; the phone is often controlled by the head of the household. In a classroom, the realistic unit is one phone per group of four, not one per child.

**Session length on the recommended board:**

| Players | @6s/turn | @10s/turn | @15s/turn |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 4.2 min | 7.1 min | 10.6 min |
| 3 | 5.6 min | 9.4 min | 14.1 min |
| 4 | 6.9 min | **11.6 min** | 17.4 min |

**Budget 10 seconds per turn and 12 minutes for a 4-player game.** That is the correct target — one chai, one classroom activity slot, one auto ride. Every second you add to the dice animation costs ~17 seconds of total session at 4 players.

**Hard rules for the handoff:**

1. **Explicit handoff card between turns.** Full-screen, player's colour + name, "PASS TO PRIYA", one big tap to continue. Without it, on a shared phone, two people roll for the same token within the first three minutes. This is the number-one pass-and-play bug and it is a UX bug, not a code bug.
2. **Animation budget ≤ 2.5s per move, tap-to-skip always on.** The phone-passing rhythm is the metronome; a 4-second snake animation on a 3D board breaks it. Long snake slides are exactly where people put the phone down.
3. **Reset the 3D camera on handoff.** If player 1 rotated the board, player 2 must not receive it upside down. Ease back to the default isometric during the handoff card — this is the single most common failure in 3D board games ported to touch.
4. **Portrait, dice button in the bottom third.** The phone is passed one-handed across a table or a desk. Anything in the top 20% of the screen is unreachable mid-pass.
5. **Names + shapes, not just colours.** "Red" and "orange" tokens are indistinguishable on a cheap LCD in Indian daylight, and someone in the group is colour-blind. Distinct token silhouettes solve both.
6. **Audio cue on snake and ladder.** In a family game half the table isn't looking at the screen. A distinct sound for "climbed" vs "bitten" keeps four people in a game only one person can see.
7. **No login, no mandatory names.** Default to Player 1–4 with an optional rename; typing four names on a shared phone is a 60-second tax before anyone has had fun. Offer rename *after* the first round.
8. **Keep it open information.** Snakes & ladders works pass-and-play precisely because there is nothing to hide. **Do not add hidden hands, secret goals, or private cards** — on a shared device they force the holder to shield the screen, which is the moment the social game dies. Every mechanic you add must survive being seen by everyone.
9. **Persistent standings strip.** The player who looked away for 30 seconds needs to re-enter without asking.
10. **Resume state on refresh.** A shared phone takes calls. Losing a 12-minute game to an incoming call from Amma will end the session permanently.

**Classroom variant:** one phone per group of 4; project nothing. The phone is the dice, the token, and the card reader. Groups finish at different times (median 26 rounds, p90 43) — so give the fast finishers the "which snake cost you most?" debrief question as the built-in end screen, and the slow groups will still be mid-game when you call time. **Design the end screen as the worksheet.**
