What is short selling?
Most investors buy shares hoping the price rises. Short sellers do the opposite — they bet the price will fall. It's legal, common among hedge funds, and very risky. Here's exactly how it works.
The gold bangles analogy 💍

Your neighbour has gold bangles worth ₹1 lakh. You think gold prices will fall, so you borrow them and sell them immediately for ₹1 lakh.

A month later, gold falls to ₹80,000. You buy new bangles for ₹80,000, return them to your neighbour, and keep ₹20,000 profit. 🎉

But what if gold rises to ₹1.5 lakh? You still owe those bangles back. You must buy at ₹1.5 lakh — a ₹50,000 loss. And if gold goes to ₹3 lakh? That's a ₹2 lakh loss... on a ₹1 lakh bet. The loss has no ceiling.
How it works in the stock market — tap each step
1
Borrow shares & sell them immediately
▶
You open a "margin account" with your broker. You borrow 10 Avis shares and sell them on the market straight away.
📥 You receive: $1,000 cash (10 shares × $100)
📋 You owe: 10 Avis shares (to be returned later)
2
Wait & watch the price
▶
You're now "short" — you want the price to drop. While you wait, you pay a small daily borrowing fee to your broker. Time is not your friend.
⏳ Every day the stock stays above $100, you're paying fees with no profit to show for it.
3
Buy back shares to "cover" your short
▶
When you're ready to close your position, you buy 10 shares on the market and return them. The difference between what you sold for and what you buy back at = your profit or loss.
✅ Bought back at $70 → Profit: ($100 − $70) × 10 = $300
❌ Bought back at $200 → Loss: ($200 − $100) × 10 = $1,000
4
The danger: the margin call
▶
If the price rises too much, your broker gets nervous. They issue a margin call — demanding you either deposit more cash immediately, or they forcibly close your position (buy back the shares on your behalf, at whatever the price is right now).
🚨 Margin call = you have no choice. You MUST buy back the shares, even if the price is $850.

Reveal the 3 squeeze ingredients one by one:
🔒
Ingredient 1: Shares locked away
SRS and Pentwater controlled 70% of the float combined — and neither wanted to lend their shares out. Short sellers could barely find stock to borrow.

The less stock available to borrow, the harder it is for shorts to close their positions when things go wrong.
📉
Ingredient 2: Heavy short interest
Many hedge funds had piled in to short Avis simultaneously. Short sellers owed back a large number of shares — shares that were already scarce to begin with.

When they'd all need to buy at once... the price had nowhere to go but up.
💥
Ingredient 3: A trigger price movement
All it took was an initial price bump — possibly some positive news or large buyers entering the market. When the price started climbing, the first margin calls went out, and the chain reaction began.

Why does scarcity make it so much worse?

When 70% of shares are locked away, even moderate panic buying causes enormous price swings. It's like 100 people trying to flee through a single door — the exit is tiny, and everyone is trapped.

The squeeze ends only when the last short seller has covered. Then the buying stops, and the price crashes back down.

3 things to take away
1
Short squeezes are always temporary
▶
The price spike is driven by panic, not company value. Once all the shorts are covered and forced buying stops, the price returns to something rational.
2
Scarcity = amplification
▶
The Avis squeeze was so extreme because 70% of shares were locked away. If shares had been freely available, the price rise would have been much smaller.
3
Short selling is not for regular investors
▶
Unlimited downside, borrowing fees, margin calls, and the risk of getting squeezed. For most investors, the risk is simply not worth it. Long-term investing with real companies is safer, simpler, and kinder to your sleep. 😊
