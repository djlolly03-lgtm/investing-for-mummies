Part one

The position

The one line everything hangs off

The only money school built for mums — taught by a business journalist who was still shut out of her own money conversations.

Two halves, and both are doing work. “For mums” is the category nobody has claimed. “A business journalist who was still shut out” is the credential that disarms — because if it happened to her, the reader stops believing her own confusion is a personal failing. That second half is not my invention: it’s the top-performing post you have ever published.

  1. 01Mums, said out loudZero of the thirteen accounts you track target mums — every one of them chases “women” broadly. This is the most ownable, least contested position in your category, and your brand name already claims it while your landing page says the generic word “programme”.
  2. 02The journalist who was still left outHiral covered business for a living and money conversations still happened over her head. That is a more persuasive credential than any certification, because it converts the reader’s shame into recognition. It is also true, differentiated, and impossible for a competitor to copy.
  3. 03A room of twelve, not a webinar of five hundredEveryone else in this category is selling Zoom. You are selling twelve women around a table in Nariman Point, in person, for eight mornings. That is the premium, and it is what justifies ₹21,000 against a ₹99 webinar. Right now the page treats it as a logistics detail.
  4. 04You play it, you don’t sit through itEleven interactive games. Not one of the thirteen tracked accounts has a single one. Your own competitor synthesis calls this a structural moat — and it is currently being used as social content rather than as the reason to hand over money or an email address.
  5. 05116 people already did thisYou have a real number and you’re hiding it. Seventy-two mums have completed the flagship. That is the honest proof point the page needs, and it is worth more than any invented statistic.
The trap to avoid

Do not position against Finkhoz on price or convenience. You will lose — they’re ₹99 and on Zoom. Position on the opposite axis: small, in person, and finite. A webinar is something you attend; this is something you finish, in a room, with eleven other women who are also starting from zero.

Testimonials — video and text

New. You have 116 alumni and zero quotes on the page. Owner: coordinator + founder · Effort: one WhatsApp round, then 20 minutes at the end of a session.

Video first — and film them in the room, on the last session. That’s the operational trick: everyone is already there, it’s the emotional peak of the course, and it costs you nothing to schedule. Trying to book alumni for filming afterwards is where most brands lose this.

The only question you need to ask on camera

Let them answer unscripted. 30–45 seconds each, shot vertical on a phone at eye level. Vertical means every clip is also a Reel — the format your own account already proves works.

Text testimonials in parallel: WhatsApp the 72 flagship alumni with the same question. Use three or four verbatim, with first name and “Flagship · [month] batch”. Aim for specifics — “I finally read my own policy documents” beats “great course”.

On the page: two or three videos, muted autoplay with captions (most people watch silent), plus three or four text quotes beneath. Faces visible in every thumbnail.

Two rules, non-negotiable

No returns or profit claims. Digital Scholar runs income-claim testimonials because it sells AI skills; you teach investing, where those edge into performance claims. Every Finkhoz testimonial is about clarity and the first action taken — not one mentions a return. Copy that discipline exactly.

Written consent before anything is published — a one-line form at filming covering web and social use. You need it under the DPDP Act, and for a brand selling trust it is cheap insurance.

Part four

Where students actually come from

Correcting my own model

I previously wrote that 342 followers implies 3–7 seats. That was the wrong model for your business, and it’s worth being precise about why, because the right model points somewhere different.

I treated Instagram as the top of your funnel. It isn’t. Your students came from Hiral’s direct network, then friends of friends, and now increasingly from people who’ve simply heard about you. Instagram isn’t where they discover you — it’s where they check you’re real after someone has already mentioned your name. On your own estimate, 50–60% of students follow the account, which means following largely happens after interest, not before it.

So a good chunk of those 342 are people you’ve already taught. Applying a follower-to-customer conversion rate to them double-counts your own customers.

The actual engineRoleStatus
Word of mouth — network → friends of friends → “heard about you”AcquisitionWorking, and widening
The landing page — where the referral landsConfirmationWeakest link
Instagram — checked after someone hears your nameValidationThin on proof
116 past students — the people doing the referringAmplifierNever asked
What this changes — and it makes the page matter more, not less

A referred visitor arrives with intent already high. Someone she trusts has told her to do this. She is not looking to be persuaded from zero — she is looking for confirmation that what she was told is true, and for the practical facts: when, how much, can I keep up.

Which means every friction point on that page costs you more, not less. She arrives ready, reads ₹19,000, and gets charged ₹21,000. She looks for the person she was told about and Hiral isn’t on the page. You are losing people at the confirmation step, which is the most expensive place to lose them — someone spent their own credibility sending her.

Three consequences follow.

  1. 01The WhatsApp share preview stops being a nice-to-haveIf word of mouth is the engine, in India that word travels on WhatsApp. Your page has no Open Graph tags, so every time someone forwards it to a friend it arrives as a naked URL — no title, no image, no description. That is your acquisition channel degrading at the exact moment of handoff. In the audit I filed this as “important”. Given how you actually grow, treat it as critical.
  2. 02Start capturing how people heard about you — the field already existsYou’re estimating 50–60% because nothing measures it. Your checkout already sends a referredBy field to your backend and it is hardcoded empty, never filled. Add one dropdown to the form — friend or family · Instagram · past student · found you online · other — and populate it. Within two cohorts you’ll know exactly which channel fills the room instead of guessing, and the answer decides where every future rupee goes.
  3. 03Ask for the referral explicitlyWord of mouth is already working without being asked for. It is the single cheapest lever you have and nobody is pulling it. Message the 72 flagship alumni — not only for testimonials, but with a plain ask and a forwardable link. A brand this early usually has to manufacture advocacy; you already have it and are leaving it passive.
On the pixel, now that there’s no ad spend

This changes the argument but not the answer. You are not wasting ad budget — but you are still failing to build the audience you’ll need the day you do spend. A Meta pixel installed today quietly collects everyone who reads this page; installed the day you launch ads, it starts from zero and you wait months. GA4 matters more now, not less: with no paid attribution, page analytics plus that referredBy field are the only way you’ll ever know what actually works.

And the strategic question

Both competitors earn a big ask with a small one first — ₹99, ₹1,997. Your cheapest entry point is ₹8,000, and it’s for teenagers. You are the only one in this comparison who could run a genuinely great free session, because you own eleven games nobody else has. A free 90-minute “play one game, learn one thing” evening — online, monthly — would build the list, the retargeting audience and the testimonial reel all at once, and it would grow the follower base that is currently the binding constraint on everything else.
